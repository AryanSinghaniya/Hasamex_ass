Expert Interview Transcript
============================
Expert Name: Dr. Jean Martin
Role: Head of Urology, CHU Bordeaux-Pellegrin; Former Clinical Advisor, HAS (Haute Autorité de Santé)
Market: France
Interview Date: 2026-09-10
Interviewer: James Whitfield, Hasamex Research

--------------------------------------------------------------------------

00:12
Interviewer: Thank you for joining us today, Dr. Martin. Can we start with adoption barriers — what are the main obstacles to robotic surgery uptake in France?

00:22
Dr. Martin: Of course. The single biggest barrier in France is capital expenditure. A da Vinci system, for example, costs somewhere between one-point-five and two million euros upfront, and that's before you factor in annual service contracts, which can run to two hundred thousand euros or more. French public hospitals — the CHUs — are under enormous fiscal pressure, so any capital item of that magnitude requires approval at the regional ARS level, sometimes all the way to the Ministry of Health. That process alone can take twelve to eighteen months.

01:05
Dr. Martin: Beyond cost, there's a genuine infrastructure challenge. Robotic systems require reinforced operating theatre flooring, dedicated electrical circuits, and often additional ventilation. Many older CHU buildings simply don't have that, and retrofitting can cost several hundred thousand euros on top of the robot itself.

01:28
Interviewer: Are there clinical-evidence barriers as well, or is the evidence base now accepted?

01:34
Dr. Martin: The evidence base is increasingly accepted for prostatectomy and certain gynaecological oncology procedures. However, for general surgery — colorectal, cholecystectomy — HAS has been quite cautious. They issued a guidance in 2024 essentially saying that robotic assistance for benign colorectal conditions is not yet proven to be cost-effective relative to standard laparoscopy. So there's a two-tier situation: urology is well-reimbursed, general surgery much less so.

02:10
Interviewer: That's a perfect segue. Can you talk about reimbursement more broadly?

02:15
Dr. Martin: Sure. France uses a GHS — groupe homogène de séjours — tariff system for hospital reimbursement, and robotic procedures do not automatically attract a higher GHS tariff than their laparoscopic equivalents. So the hospital bears the additional cost of the robot and consumables — about fifteen hundred to two thousand euros per procedure in disposables alone — without receiving additional reimbursement. This is the fundamental economic tension. The only procedures with some dedicated recognition are radical prostatectomy and certain partial nephrectomies, where the clinical evidence is strongest.

03:01
Dr. Martin: There is some movement. The CEPS — the pricing committee — has been in discussions about whether to create a specific tariff supplement for robotic procedures in urology and gynaecological oncology. If that goes through, which I'd estimate has maybe a sixty percent probability over the next two years, it could be a significant catalyst.

03:25
Interviewer: Let's turn to competitive dynamics. How would you characterise the landscape among the major platforms?

03:32
Dr. Martin: Intuitive Surgical — da Vinci — is still dominant, probably holding around seventy-five percent of installed base in France. But the competitive dynamics are shifting. Medtronic's Hugo system received CE mark, and we've seen a handful of installations at major centres. Stryker's Mako is entrenched in orthopaedics. The more interesting development is the entry of CMR Surgical's Versius, which is a modular, smaller-footprint system. A couple of teaching hospitals in Lyon and Nantes have trialled it specifically because it fits into existing theatres without major retrofitting.

04:15
Dr. Martin: The key competitive differentiators, from conversations with procurement colleagues, are: one, total cost of ownership — not just the sticker price but service, consumables, financing; two, the training ecosystem — if a surgeon has trained on da Vinci, switching platforms is a significant barrier; and three, data and connectivity, because hospital IT departments are increasingly asking about integration with Epic or local EMR systems.

04:45
Interviewer: On training — what does that pathway look like in France?

04:51
Dr. Martin: In France, there is no formal national credentialing requirement for robotic surgery. Training is largely platform-specific and vendor-driven. Intuitive runs a structured programme — the da Vinci certification pathway — that includes simulation, supervised cases, and a case log requirement. This is good but it creates platform lock-in, because a surgeon who has done fifty cases on da Vinci is not going to walk away from that credential to start over on Hugo.

05:25
Dr. Martin: There's a growing push from the French Society of Urology and the French Society of General Surgery to develop competency-based, platform-agnostic credentialing. But as of mid-2026, it's still largely aspirational. The practical bottleneck today is simulator access — many smaller hospitals don't have simulators, so trainees must travel to reference centres, which slows the pipeline.

05:58
Interviewer: Moving to procurement — how do hospitals actually make the buying decision?

06:04
Dr. Martin: It's almost always a multi-stakeholder process. You have the clinical champion — typically the head of urology or surgical oncology — who initiates the business case. Then the hospital CFO and the purchase department get involved, and in public hospitals, there's a mandatory public tender process above a certain threshold. For a multi-million-euro system, that's always a formal appel d'offres — public tender — which by law must be open for a minimum period and scored on defined criteria. This makes the timeline long: typically eighteen to twenty-four months from initial proposal to contract signature.

06:48
Dr. Martin: What's changed in the last two years is the emergence of pay-per-procedure and lease models. Several vendors are now offering programmes where the capital cost is minimal — sometimes zero — and the hospital pays a fixed amount per procedure performed, bundled with consumables and service. For a hospital that cannot justify capital expenditure, this is very attractive. I'd estimate roughly a third of new installations in France in 2025 were on some kind of usage-based model rather than outright purchase.

07:22
Interviewer: Finally, what's your three-to-five year outlook for robotic surgery in France?

07:28
Dr. Martin: I'm optimistic but with caveats. I think the installed base will roughly double by 2030, from the current estimated two hundred and twenty systems to something like four hundred and fifty. The growth will be driven primarily by urology and gynaecological oncology in the near term, and I expect colorectal to follow if the evidence strengthens. The single biggest accelerator would be a positive reimbursement decision for a robotic tariff supplement. If that doesn't happen, growth will be slower — maybe forty percent rather than a hundred percent over five years.

08:05
Dr. Martin: On technology, the next generation of systems with integrated AI — things like real-time tissue identification, autonomous camera control — will be important for justifying the cost premium. I'm particularly watching the haptic feedback developments; that's been a longstanding criticism of robotic surgery, and if vendors can crack it, it will significantly improve the clinical case.

08:32
Interviewer: That's very comprehensive. Thank you, Dr. Martin.

08:36
Dr. Martin: My pleasure.
